A project page should help a reader understand the work without having to open every notebook. The detail can live in the repository; the page provides a clear route through it.

This is an example article and a starting structure for future project notes. Replace the prompts with your own experience before publishing it as personal writing.

## Lead with the question and result

Open with the problem, the intended audience, and the most useful conclusion. If the work is exploratory or unfinished, say so. An honest account of a useful deliverable is stronger than an unsupported claim of impact.

Add a short account of one of your own projects here: what was the question, and what did your work make possible?

## Explain the choices that mattered

Readers need enough method to assess the result. Explain why you selected a comparison, excluded certain records, or defined a metric in a particular way. Link to the full code for implementation details.

Useful details include:

- The source, date range, and unit of analysis.
- A preparation step that changed the interpretation.
- A check you used to catch an error or challenge an assumption.
- A tradeoff you would revisit with more time or better data.

## Give every figure a job

Choose charts that support a specific statement. Provide units, a readable legend when needed, and a caption explaining what the reader should notice. Include a text explanation so the conclusion remains accessible without the graphic.

Insert a figure from your own work and explain why this particular view was helpful.

## Make the work reproducible

Describe how to obtain the data, install the required dependencies, and run the analysis. Keep private or restricted information out of a public repository, and explain any access requirements for data you cannot share.

## Keep the limits visible

Close with the main uncertainty and the next useful step. The reader should be able to distinguish what you measured, what you inferred, and what still needs to be tested.
