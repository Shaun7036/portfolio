An analysis is easier to judge when its question is clear. “Explore sales” describes an activity. “Which change best explains the difference in sales between these two periods?” gives the work a direction.

This sample article demonstrates the writing format. Replace it with your own perspective, examples, and evidence before presenting it as your work.

## Start with a decision

Write down who will use the result and what they could do differently. If the answer would not change a decision, explain what understanding the analysis is intended to build instead.

A useful starting sentence is:

> This analysis helps [audience] decide [decision] by understanding [question].

## Define the comparison

A number rarely explains much in isolation. Identify the baseline, period, population, and unit before choosing a chart. Ask whether a difference in totals reflects a real change or simply a different number of observations.

For a project article, add an example of a vague question you refined. Explain which definition made the biggest difference to the analysis.

## Decide what would count as evidence

List the data needed to answer the question and the gaps that would weaken a conclusion. Separate a pattern you can observe from a cause you would need a stronger design to establish.

Describe one validation check from your own project here. A small check that changed your interpretation can tell the reader more than a long list of tools.

## End with the next step

Finish by stating what the reader should take away, what remains uncertain, and what you would examine next. A well-framed question makes that ending much easier to write.
